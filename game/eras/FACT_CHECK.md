# Era decks: what to check before print

Every era card is on COMMUNITY.md's verify-before-printing list. This page is the historian's and the financial reviewer's starting point: what each card writer flagged as not fully sure. **All dollar figures are plausible estimates, not sourced**; each card's `basis` says how it was reckoned, so check those first. No card names a real business. Where a card stays generic on purpose ("the plant", "the foundry"), that's noted.

## Chapter 1, *Freedom's Ledger* (`ch1.py`, Lending and Labor, 1865–1877)
1. **The Freedman's Savings Bank.** The card says depositors "lost most of what they had". The usual figures are about 61,000 depositors and about $3 million, with later partial payouts of up to about 62% to those who claimed; "much of" may be more accurate. Frederick Douglass (its last president) isn't on the card for space; add him if wanted. "Deposit as little as you have" stands for the small minimums (about 5 cents).
2. **Store accounts:** interest charged on top of credit prices and figured at settlement; the effective rates (often quoted at 40–60% a year or more) aren't on the card.
3. **Lien laws** putting the merchant first: landlord versus merchant priority varied by state and over time.
4. **Carried-over debt** binding a family to the same land: the practice is documented; how far the law enforced it varied by state.
5. **Fertilizer on credit:** widespread in Georgia and the Carolinas in the 1870s, less so elsewhere.
6. **Fake land titles** and the land promised in 1865 (Field Order No. 15) going back to the old owners: the painted-stakes and fake-deed sales are reported, mostly as anecdotes.
7. **The Black Codes:** the January contract deadline (Mississippi, 1865) and the claim that federal law had set most codes aside by 1867; apprenticing children to former owners (Mississippi, Maryland); vagrancy fines paid off by being hired out; enticement laws (the line that planters "kept wages down together" through them is an interpretation).
8. **Losing the whole year's pay** for leaving early: in many contracts and some codes.
9. **Labor agents paid by the head** to carry families "out west" (Louisiana, Mississippi, Arkansas, Texas) in the early 1870s.
10. **Freedmen's Bureau agents witnessing contracts:** mainly 1865–1868, and some agents sided with planters.
11. **Families bargaining as groups** (documented by Foner); check the "planters had to listen" framing.
12. **Benevolent societies** choosing their own officers through the church; **families buying land together** with a recorded deed (documented in places, not everywhere).
13. Convict leasing was left out on purpose; the nearest card is a vagrancy fine worked off for the planter who pays it.

## Chapter 2, *Up North* (`ch2.py`, Labor and Housing, 1910s–1940s)
1. **Employment offices splitting fees with foremen:** documented around 1910–1915 (US Commission on Industrial Relations); no Dayton source.
2. **Company store orders / scrip:** best documented in railroad camps and company towns, not Dayton factories. Consider setting the card "on the way north", or cutting it.
3. **Labor agents paid by both the worker and the company:** common in the general record; details varied.
4. **Strikebreaking:** Black migrants recruited without being told (1919 steel strike, the Chicago stockyards); no Dayton case.
5. **The company "club"** (picnics, ball team, insurance) to keep unions out: 1920s welfare capitalism. Dayton's best-known example is a named company, so the card stays generic.
6. **Yellow-dog contracts:** upheld from *Hitchman* (1917) until the Norris–LaGuardia Act (1932).
7. **The CIO card:** dues of about $1 a month and organizing "Black and white together" fit 1935 on, the late end of the chapter; not every local was equal in practice.
8. **"The colored branch of the Y":** Dayton is believed to have had a Black YMCA branch on West Fifth Street; its dates and whether it listed jobs and rooms are unconfirmed (the card doesn't name it).
9. **A Black-run building and loan** in Dayton, 1910–1940: unconfirmed (the card is generic).
10. **Lodge sick-and-burial benefits** (Prince Hall Masons, the Black Elks, the Odd Fellows' Black branch): well documented in general; Dayton chapters not checked.
11. **Covenants:** enforced from *Corrigan v. Buckley* (1926) until *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948); whether Dayton deeds carried them is unchecked, and "across the river" is loose geography.
12. **The realtors' code:** the 1924 NAREB Code of Ethics, Article 34, paraphrased; check the wording.
13. **Black tenants paying more for worse housing:** documented nationally (Chicago, Cleveland); no Dayton rent figures.
14. **Key money:** best documented in the WWII housing shortage (late in the chapter). **"Kitchenette"** is mainly a Chicago word.

## Chapter 4, *Voice* (`ch4.py`, Labor and Education, 1955–1970)
1. **Ohio had no poll tax or literacy test.** The clerk's "application" card (Education) is now set down South (a letter from cousins) and names the Voting Rights Act (1965) that ended the tests: check that framing. The registration-hours card is generic (Montgomery County's hours unconfirmed).
2. **Household workers' minimum wage:** the card says the federal wage law left them out; FLSA coverage came in 1974.
3. **Central State University:** state-supported from 1947, a university from 1965; the NDEA loan terms (1958: low interest, repayment after school, part forgiven for teachers); "near Xenia". Naming it needs permission (COMMUNITY.md §4).
4. **Civil rights complaints:** the Ohio Civil Rights Commission (believed 1959) and the EEOC (1965); no filing deadline is named.
5. **Departmental seniority** keeping Black workers in the worst jobs (*Quarles v. Philip Morris*, 1968) and **apprenticeships restricted to members' relatives:** documented nationally; Dayton plants and halls unconfirmed.
6. **Freedom of choice** struck down in *Green v. County School Board* (1968); the office-hours detail is generic.
7. **Sanitation crews sent home for rain without pay** echoes Memphis, 1968; not a Dayton claim.
8. **Freedom schools and citizenship classes:** the best known were in Mississippi (1964) and run by the SCLC and Highlander; whether Dayton had a school boycott with a freedom school needs checking if the card should read as local.
9. Salary figures (a principal's $7,000, a union staff job's $5,000) are guesses.

## Chapter 5, *The Plants Close* (`ch5.py`, Housing and Labor, 1990s–2009) — also for the financial reviewer
1. **Ohio's answer deadline** in a foreclosure ("28 days after you're served", Civil Rule 12(A)): confirm.
2. **Severance releases at 40 and over:** the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act gives 21 days (45 in a group layoff) and 7 days to revoke; the card says "weeks" on purpose.
3. **The WARN Act:** 60 days' notice and back pay if skipped (right); the size limits (100+ employees, 50+ laid off at a site) aren't on the card.
4. **Pensions after bankruptcy:** the PBGC pays only up to a cap, and retiree benefits can change in bankruptcy; a Delphi/GM example would need its own check.
5. **Trade Adjustment Assistance:** whether Moraine or NCR workers were certified is unknown; the Moraine closure was about SUV demand, not imports, so the card says "if trade cost your job".
6. **Reverse redlining** (lenders steering Black neighborhoods to subprime, even borrowers who qualified for prime): backed by research and DOJ settlements (Countrywide 2011, Wells Fargo 2012); keep a source on file.
7. **Force-placed insurance** commissions to servicers ("often"), and **single-premium credit insurance** financed into the loan (mostly gone by 2000–2002): need sources.
8. **401(k) matches in company stock** for the employers in mind; the **2/28 loan** adjusting every six months after month 24 with a penalty outlasting the reset (typical, not universal).
9. Dollar figures ($25,000 of equity in a West Side house, a $9/hr temp wage, $300/month payment jumps) should be checked against Dayton data from 2005–2009.
